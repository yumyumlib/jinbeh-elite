# The Stories of Jinbeh: Heritage, Dishes & Soul of a Family Restaurant

*Compiled from 25 training recordings (audio & video), October 6-7, 2025*
*Frisco & Lewisville, Texas*

---

## Part One: The Origin Story

### A Father's Vision (1988)

Jinbeh Japanese Restaurant was founded in 1988 in Las Colinas, Texas. The founder -- a Japanese man who, remarkably, never cooked a day in his life -- opened a restaurant not out of culinary ambition but out of an entrepreneurial instinct and a desire to bring authentic Japanese dining to North Texas. He set up the systems, hired the team, and then largely stepped back. He was rarely seen at the restaurant; he'd come in from time to time to chat, ride himself around the place, but the day-to-day was always in the hands of the people he trusted.

His philosophy was simple: be humble, hardworking, and honest. These three words became the spiritual foundation of Jinbeh and were passed down to his son, who now runs both locations.

### The Las Colinas Years (1988-2010s)

The original Las Colinas location was a true Japanese kitchen. The menu was a book -- thick, ambitious, and authentically Japanese. It featured dishes that only Japanese customers would order, like salted mackerel and traditional set menus. Over time, the restaurant learned a critical lesson: the menu needed to cater to the American palate while preserving Japanese soul. The best-selling items that resonated with the broader North Texas demographic were kept; the ultra-traditional items that only a niche audience ordered were retired.

Las Colinas was a corporate area. During the day on Saturdays and Sundays, the restaurant was closed -- there was no lunch crowd on weekends. They only opened for dinner. This gave the team bandwidth to participate in festivals and community events, which became a signature part of Jinbeh's identity.

The Las Colinas location also had something special: proximity to the Dallas Cowboys' practice facility. For a solid ten years (roughly 2010-2020), Cowboys players became regulars and friends. They attended the restaurant's last day when Las Colinas eventually closed. One notable regular was Ezekiel Elliott, who came in frequently before being dropped from the team. The signed photographs that now hang in the Frisco location were brought over from Las Colinas as mementos of that era.

The owner describes those years with Cowboys players as a privilege: the parties were different, the energy was electric, and the relationships were genuine. He even received invitations to attend games overseas during international tours.

### The Newspaper Article

A customer who was also a writer came to dine at Jinbeh and fell in love with the family restaurant aspect and its community impact. She asked to write about it. The resulting article captured the owner's honest story about doing everything for his father -- being humble, hardworking, and honest. A regular customer was so moved by the article that they had it framed and gifted it to the restaurant, where it still hangs today.

## Part Two: The People of Jinbeh

### Ingrid: The True Horn Leader

Ingrid is described as the heart of Jinbeh's front-of-house operations. She is respected across both locations -- not just Frisco, but Lewisville too. The staff follows her because she's honest, hardworking, and always goes the extra mile. When she's out serving, the other staff watches her as a model. She sets rules and they're followed, not because of authority but because of love and respect.

Her responsibilities include payroll, scheduling, and training. She handles many of the things the owner doesn't enjoy doing. She's described as a much better trainer than the owner himself. Her strength is that the respect is so deep that she can get the team to follow her lead on anything.

### Fujisan: The Sushi Master (30 Years)

Fujisan -- the head sushi chef -- has been with Jinbeh for nearly 30 years, possibly more. He started long ago and is described as "my second dad" by the owner. The entire sushi menu was a dual collaboration between Fujisan and the owner -- sleepless nights working together to craft and refine every roll, every presentation.

He is from the south side of Japan, a region known for its noodles. When he was at the Las Colinas location, he created a ramen section called "Fuji San Ramen" that was extremely popular. The owner would love to bring it back.

In Japanese culture, golf is a bonding ritual. You can say whatever you want -- even complain about your boss -- while playing golf or drinking, and it's honored without judgment. The way to have a deep conversation with Fujisan is to take him golfing.

### The Longest-Tenured Employee

The oldest employee has been with Jinbeh since 1988 -- since the very beginning. He started as a dishwasher, became a busser, and has been a cornerstone ever since. The second-oldest just started around the same era. Both are legendary within the organization. The senior employee handles food strategy and logistics for events like festivals -- he doesn't manage people, but he's the mastermind behind the food operations.

## Part Three: The Menu -- Stories Behind the Dishes

### The Philosophy: American Authentic

Jinbeh's menu philosophy emerged from decades of trial and error. The original Las Colinas menu was a thick book of truly authentic Japanese dishes. Over time, they learned: people want authentic, but they want American authentic. The sweet spot is food that feels genuinely Japanese enough that customers feel transported, but not so foreign that they're uncomfortable.

As the owner puts it: "We've learned to just kind of cave in, because at first we were like, no, it isn't like that." Through the Las Colinas location, they learned that ultra-authentic dishes (like the traditional condiments -- great ginger, radish, scallion, bonito flakes) don't resonate the same way with the Texas demographic.

The really authentic Japanese restaurants know this too -- that's why they have eight seats and a set menu. Jinbeh chose a different path: family-friendly, accessible, and large enough to serve the community.

### Appetizers: The Stories

**Spicy Garlic Edamame** -- Regular edamame gets an upgrade. Sauteed with spicy sauce and garlic, given a little burnt flavor on purpose. Upsold for an extra dollar. One of those items where the preparation makes a humble ingredient feel special.

**Shishito Peppers** -- Japanese peppers that look like jalapenos but aren't spicy. Served with homemade ponzu sauce (a lemony soy-based sauce). A healthy, authentic option.

**Beef Tataki** -- In Japanese, "tataki" means "to hit" or "to pound" -- like tataki massage. The beef is tenderized by pounding it thin, then barely seared on the outside so it's more rare. A very traditional Japanese dish served with house ponzu sauce. Super thin slices.

**Karaage (Japanese Fried Chicken)** -- Properly pronounced "kara-geh," though many people say "kara-age." In Japanese, "age" means fried; "kara" means spicy. In Japan, it's traditionally served with a mustard paste. Jinbeh removed the mustard because only Japanese customers liked it -- the American palate prefers sweeter, sugary sauces. The karaage is marinated (unlike the chicken tenders), and if you try them back-to-back, most people prefer the marinated version. But parents order chicken tenders for kids because it feels safer.

**Tempura vs. Agedashi** -- Both involve frying, but tempura uses a much thicker batter and is deep fried, while "age" (as in agedashi) is lighter. The vegetable tempura comes with warm tempura sauce -- a light, watery broth. The agedashi tofu is deep fried with a very light batter, then soaked in sauce so it's crispy and soupy simultaneously. The sauce becomes the dominant flavor since tofu is so mild.

**Soft Shell Crab** -- A whole crab, about palm-sized, battered, deep fried, and chopped into four or five pieces. Delicious and impressive.

**Korokke (Croquettes)** -- Very traditional Japanese, similar to Spanish croquetas. A potato base with a bit of meat inside, served with tonkatsu sauce. "Like the winner" -- meaning it's a winner dish. Especially good in winter.

**Haramaki (Spring Rolls)** -- Made in-house, which is time-consuming. The kitchen makes the mix and rolls them up weekly. They're small, which occasionally generates comments, but the price reflects it. People who try one always order again.

**Calamari Rings** -- The one appetizer they don't make in-house. Comes pre-made in a bag and is simply deep fried. But customers love the specific brand they use.


### Sushi: The Art & Terminology

**Sushi vs. Sashimi vs. Maki:**
- **Sashimi** = just the raw fish slices, no rice. The word means "sliced."
- **Sushi (Nigiri)** = rice ball with fish on top. The proper name is "nigiri-zushi."
- **Maki** = rolled sushi, using the bamboo roller (makisu). Rice on inside or outside -- they're all maki as long as the roller is used.

The owner tried putting pictures of sushi on the menu once. The feedback was devastating: "This is very Chinese restaurant... low-grade Chinese restaurant." High-end Japanese restaurants don't do picture menus. They removed them. However, there might be value in having a separate lookbook or spiral binder at the sushi bar for curious customers.

**The Sashimi Sampler** -- Three most popular fish (tuna, salmon, and one more) at a discount. Only available on the appetizer menu, not duplicated in the sushi section. A better deal than ordering individual sashimi portions.

**The Sushi Sampler** -- One piece of each popular fish on rice, discounted to encourage people to try the flavors.

### Sauces: The Secret Weapons

**Yum Yum Sauce** -- An in-house mayo-based sauce: mayo + a little sriracha + a ton of sugar. Light orange color. This is probably the biggest draw on a hibachi plate. People are genuinely addicted to it.

**Teriyaki Sauce** -- Homemade. "This is where people think Japanese is healthy, but when you put all these sauces on it..." The kids love it on yakitori.

**Ponzu Sauce** -- House-made, lemony soy-based. Paired with shishito peppers and tataki dishes.

**Tonkatsu Sauce** -- The thick brown sweet sauce served with croquettes and certain fried items. Similar in appearance to teriyaki but with more vinegar. Kids love it.

**The Blaster Sauce** -- Jinbeh's "spicy" option, but honestly not that spicy. Even non-spicy customers handle it fine. For the truly spicy-obsessed, they keep a crazy spicy paste made from a house sriracha-togarashi mix.

All sauces are made in-house. The recipes are typed out and hung on the walls in the kitchen. Every cook learns them. The head chefs oversee quality, but training is family-style -- everyone jumps in and helps.

### Hibachi: Theater & Technique

**The Grill Operations:**
- Grills must be turned on as soon as customers are seated -- they take about 10 minutes to reach temperature. The Frisco grills take even longer because they're electric (Lewisville uses gas).
- Temperature reaches approximately 550 degrees Fahrenheit.
- At that temperature, cross-contamination from allergens is extremely difficult. Still, chefs are trained to handle allergy situations carefully -- cooking allergen items first or last and cleaning spatulas thoroughly.
- Gluten-free is easy: it's only the sauces that contain gluten, not the base ingredients. Fried rice is easily made gluten-free.

**The Seating System:**
The hibachi side runs on a manual tracking system that has been tradition since the very beginning. A paper sheet tracks table numbers, party sizes, seating times, and rounds. Tables are used in a specific priority order (front tables first, back tables less frequently). Critical rules: customers should never wait more than 10 minutes at lunch, 15 at dinner. When a table is closed for new seating, it's circled on the sheet. On crazy weekends, they fill four or more rounds.

**The Chateau Briand Cut:**
When a customer orders steak at the hibachi, they don't tell the server the temperature -- the chef gets that information directly. The ordering system uses numbered temperatures (1 through 8 for doneness).

**Grill Maintenance:**
Chefs pick two tables on a random weekday (usually Monday or Tuesday) for deep cleaning. They clean the grills, wipe the walls, and maintain the equipment. The trap under each grill catches eggshells and debris and is cleaned nightly.

### Fried Rice: The Hidden Vegetables

The fried rice contains finely chopped scallion, regular onion, and carrots -- but they're cut so small you can't see them. This is deliberate: customers (especially kids) don't want to taste or see vegetables. Jinbeh got feedback early on that there were too many visible vegetables. Now the chopping is so fine it's invisible. The Chinese restaurant comparison: some places put no vegetables at all. Jinbeh finds the middle ground.

**The Fried Rice Surcharge:**
When sitting at the hibachi, fried rice is included in the experience. But if a sushi bar customer orders fried rice, it's priced as a standalone dish -- not discounted. The most commonly ordered side from hibachi customers is chicken fried rice, by a massive margin (90% of the time).

### Holiday Pricing: The Swap

For major holidays (Christmas Eve, Christmas Day, Mother's Day, Valentine's Day, etc.), Jinbeh swaps out the regular menus entirely. An in-house designer creates themed menus with seasonal backgrounds -- snowflakes for winter, flowers for Mother's Day, etc. The holiday menus are physically swapped in and the regular menus removed so customers don't feel a direct comparison.

After the holiday, everything goes back to normal. The POS system can be programmed with timed price changes (e.g., "start at 10 AM on December 24th"). The regular menu prices are raised no more than every two years -- the owner tries not to go past that. It's all about inflation, watching invoices, food costs, and customer pain tolerance.

## Part Four: The Bar -- Sake, Spirits & Stories

### Sake: The Great Misconception

The truth about sake quality:
- **The good stuff is always cold.** Premium sake performs better at cooler temperatures.
- **The process:** Rice is polished over and over, each pass producing a finer grade. The more polished, the higher quality. Hot sake uses the unpolished leftovers.
- **The $60 unfiltered bottle:** Must be shaken before pouring. The white cloud inside is rice sediment -- a sign of the unfiltered process.
- **Less headache:** Higher quality sake (the cold, bottled kind) produces fewer headaches the next day. Lower quality creates worse hangovers.

Jinbeh tried expanding its sake selection. It didn't work in Frisco -- customers want what they know and don't want more variation. In Las Colinas, the corporate crowd appreciated variety, but the Frisco family demographic does not.

### Wine: Keep It Familiar

The wine strategy is minimalist by design. Jinbeh has tried fancier wines -- they didn't sell. The insight: customers trust Jinbeh's expertise in sake, not wine. This isn't a French or Italian restaurant. So the wine selection stays with recognizable, mass-advertised names. Most customers order house wine and are perfectly happy with it.

Red wines are served at room temperature and benefit from aeration (breathing). White wines and cold sake must be refrigerated. These are small details that the staff learns for those customers who do appreciate wine.

### The Vodka Strategy

The bar keeps a large vodka selection because it's the most popular spirit. The well (cheap) vodka costs about $5 a bottle and is charged at $8.50 per drink. When customers say "just whatever," they get the well -- and that's the highest margin drink.

But there's an art to reading customers: if someone has a nice watch, don't insult them with the cheap stuff. Offer Grey Goose. If they look budget-conscious, don't push the expensive bottles. It takes time to develop this people-reading skill.

### The Hand-Painted Tequila Bottles

One of the most visually striking features behind the bar: a collection of hand-painted tequila bottles from a Mexican brand where every bottle is individually painted by artisans. The brand employs an entire village in Mexico. It's very high-quality tequila, and the bottles are kept as decoration even after they're empty. It took about a year to accumulate enough empty bottles to line the shelf. Now that the shelf is full, extra empties go to kitchen staff who take them home to decorate their houses.

### Japanese Whisky: The Rare Hunt

Jinbeh stocks Yamazaki and other Japanese whiskeys, including some that are increasingly rare. Japanese distilleries are sending fewer pallets to the US, and high-end steakhouses snap up the allocations first. Jinbeh's most expensive pour is $35 an ounce -- a bottle that the owner has to "beg and pull teeth" to acquire. It doesn't sell often, but it looks prestigious on the shelf and the customers who know their whisky deeply appreciate it.

Whisky is served with a special large ice cube that melts slowly to avoid diluting the drink. The owner has seen custom ice molds that can engrave a logo -- a Jinbeh-branded ice cube would be a memorable touch for premium whisky service.

### The Glass-Washing Ritual

Health code requires a three-step process for bar glassware: wash, rinse, sanitize. The sanitizing solution uses quaternary ammonium chloride tablets. All wine glasses and delicate stemware are hand-washed (never put in the dishwasher -- the stems are too fragile). After sanitizing, glasses are polished by hand when mostly dry. The bar area must always be set up with the three-sink system when health inspectors visit.

## Part Five: Operations & Traditions

### The Birthday Protocol (Three Touches)

Birthday celebrations follow a precise three-contact system:
1. **The host** -- First to acknowledge. When checking in, the host identifies the birthday person, writes a description (e.g., "BD, red hair, seat 3"), and personally says happy birthday.
2. **The server** -- Notified by the host. The birthday person's seat number is circled on the order sheet.
3. **The chef** -- Informed that there's a birthday at the table.

The customer should hear "happy birthday" three separate times from three different staff members. At the end of dinner, the team brings out ice cream with tambourines and noisemakers for a celebration. Front-of-house staff joins the server to deliver the dessert.

### The Reservation & Walk-In System

Jinbeh uses OpenTable for reservations but maintains a parallel paper system for walk-ins and communication. The paper system uses specific codes:
- **Star** = reservation
- **No star** = walk-in
- **"Call ahead"** = customer phoned ahead

Each entry records: name, party size, arrival time, quoted wait time, and notes (birthday, special occasion, etc.). When a call-ahead arrives and checks in, their entry is scratched. When seated, it gets crossed out. These small visual signals keep the entire front-of-house team synchronized without verbal communication.

Reservations take priority over walk-ins, but if a reservation is late (10-15 minutes), walk-ins who arrived first will be seated. Every situation is judged individually.

### The Customer Count Tradition

Since the very beginning, Jinbeh has tracked daily hibachi customer counts by hand. A calendar-style grid tracks Monday through Sunday, with the number of people seated at hibachi each day. Little notes capture anomalies: daddy-daughter dance, Valentine's Day weekend, cheer competitions. The owner describes this as writing "notes for our future selves" -- so next year's team can understand patterns.

These records go back years. Pre-2020 records are archived. The post-COVID years were especially interesting -- when stimulus checks went out, the restaurant was slammed for an entire year.

### The VIP "Park" Program

For long-time regulars, Jinbeh maintains a behind-the-scenes loyalty approach. Through OpenTable's notes system, staff can see how many times a customer has visited, their history of no-shows and cancellations, birthdays, and any general notes. Difficult customers get noted too -- not to refuse service, but to ensure they don't get priority over loyal regulars who have supported the restaurant for years.

### The Festival History

When Jinbeh was in Las Colinas (closed Saturdays and Sundays during the day), the team could do festivals. They participated in community events for years, including a cancer charity beer crawl where a team of 20 would dress in themed costumes (superheroes one year, old people another). Jinbeh sponsored by feeding all the volunteers -- trays of fried rice -- in exchange for logo placement on event shirts.

The challenge now: festivals happen on weekends, and both locations use all their staff on weekends. But because of Jinbeh's deep relationships with former employees, the owner can call on five or more people who'd happily come back to help.

### Holiday Closures

Jinbeh is closed January 1st -- a traditional Japanese custom. Last year it fell on a Friday and the owner wished they had been open because many customers showed up expecting them to be open. The lesson: if New Year's Day falls on a weekend, consider opening.

Thanksgiving: closed just the day before. The crew works through the holiday season. December is when they need everybody -- nobody takes leave in December.

### The Copycat Restaurant

A restaurant in Lewisville literally took Jinbeh's menu -- the same exact thing -- removed the name and put their own on it. The owner's response was philosophical: "If it's going to happen, it's going to happen. Somebody really knows." The copycat restaurant didn't survive past five years. "Hey, karma."

### Food Safety Certifications

Managers must pass a food safety certification test (ServSafe or equivalent) -- an in-person, hours-long exam at a testing center. The owner describes sending one employee who failed multiple times before finally passing. At least one certified manager must be present during health inspections. The certificate is good for five years. All other employees need a basic food handler certificate.

### The Plateware Records

The owner keeps a detailed record of every plate order -- the company (Koyo), item numbers, prices per plate, and quantities ordered per location. At the Lewisville sushi bar, there are mats showing all the plate types and prices. This comes in handy when reordering.

### The Bar Test

New employees must pass a bar test created by the owner. It covers knowledge of spirits, sake, wine, and cocktail preparation. "They need to pass this bar test."

## Part Six: The Dishes They Want to Bring Back

### Fuji San Ramen
Created by Fujisan (the head sushi chef from the south of Japan, a region famous for noodles). Had its own section on the Las Colinas menu and was extremely popular. The owner would love to bring this back.

### Teppanyaki (Toban Yaki)
A clay pot with a ceramic dish on top, cooked over an open flame. The presentation was beautiful -- it came with a lid that you opened in front of the customer. Super garlicky, topped with shimai (similar to what goes on steak). Sold very well at Las Colinas.

### Takoyaki
Yoko brought this back at the Lewisville location on a trial basis. These are Japanese octopus balls -- a popular street food.

## Part Seven: The Failed Experiments

Not everything worked. The restaurant has tried and abandoned:

- **Hibachi tacos** -- Didn't stick
- **Changing the soup** -- Customers revolted; they want what they know
- **Fancy wines** -- Corporate Las Colinas crowd liked variety; Frisco families do not
- **Expanded sake selection** -- Same story; customers want the familiar
- **Picture menus** -- Perceived as "low-grade Chinese restaurant"
- **New/seasonal menu items** -- The Frisco customer base does not want novelty. They don't want a special dumpling just for December. They want the same reliable experience every time.

The lesson: Jinbeh's customers are extraordinarily resistant to change. They come for consistency, familiarity, and the feeling of being known.

## Part Eight: The Soul of the Place

### The Kids Strategy

Jinbeh is intensely family-focused. They fold origami cranes and hand them out to every child from a basket. Training chopsticks are available for little ones. Lollipops are distributed. The philosophy: invest in the kids, and they grow up saying "I want to go to Jinbeh." They hear stories of children who started coming as toddlers and now bring their own friends.

### The Zero-Waste Philosophy

The little chicken rolls were invented because a hibachi chef asked, "Why not use the whole chicken?" Sauces are made from scratch to control quality. Even the hand-painted tequila bottles get a second life as decoration.

### The Family Culture

Training at Jinbeh is described as family-style. There's no formal corporate training program -- the head chefs train new cooks, but really, everyone jumps in. New servers need Japanese restaurant experience (knowing sushi names is essential and takes a long time to learn), but once they're in, the culture absorbs them.

The kitchen staff career path is clear: start as a dishwasher, become a busser, then potentially move to kitchen chef, and eventually to hibachi chef. But hibachi chefs are specialized -- they stay in their domain. Same for sushi chefs. The front of house is more flexible: bartenders can serve, servers can bus, everyone adapts.

### The Philosophy on Competition

When asked about protecting recipes and knowledge from employees who leave, the owner is pragmatic: "It's too late for that." The menu is on the internet. People have stolen it. But loyalty to the brand, pricing discipline, and decades of built relationships are what survive. A copycat can take your menu but can't replicate thirty-seven years of community trust.

### The Honest Business

The philosophy: humble, hardworking, honest.
